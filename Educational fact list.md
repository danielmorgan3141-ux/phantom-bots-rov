# Educational narration facts

1. R-O-V stands for remotely operated vehicle. A person controls its movements from a distance. In this game, removing quagga mussels and collecting eDNA samples earns points. Movement and tools use battery power, and the vehicle recharges at the surface station.

2. Quagga mussels were confirmed in nearby Geneva Lake, Wisconsin, in 2024. The information used for our project says they have only spread to part of Geneva Lake. We want to find out whether something is limiting their spread, or whether they will reach the rest of the lake with time. Understanding that could help protect other nearby lakes. The game uses a simplified lake that gets bigger with every level.

3. Zebra and quagga mussels can look very similar. Zebra mussels have a flatter underside and can stand upright on it. Quaggas have a rounded underside and tend to tip onto their side. Machine learning can help researchers classify images. This game’s three-second identification is a simulation, not a real machine learning analysis.

4. Zebra mussels are already widespread in Geneva Lake, while quagga mussels arrived more recently. Our project prioritizes containing the newer quagga invasion while its spread may still be limited. That is why zebra mussels earn no points in this game. Both species can damage lake ecosystems.

5. Mussels are filter feeders. Quagga mussels eat algae and tiny plankton from the water. Large populations can remove food that other animals need and change the lake’s food web.

6. Clearer water does not always mean a healthier lake. Heavy mussel filtering can make water clearer while reducing the tiny organisms that support other wildlife.

7. Quagga mussels can live on hard or soft surfaces. Unlike the zebras restricted to rocky beds in this game, real quaggas can also spread across softer lake bottoms.

8. eDNA means environmental DNA. Organisms leave genetic material behind in water through waste and shed cells. Researchers can test water samples for traces of a target species.

9. Baby mussels, called veligers, drift through the water before settling. Our project uses eDNA samples to look for mussel DNA as an early warning that they may be spreading, even before new adult mussels are easy to see.

10. In our mission, use eDNA to monitor the possible spread of baby mussels. Sample near and beyond the known mussel area, at different depths, and compare results over time. This helps researchers decide where to search more closely for a new infestation.

11. An eDNA test detects mussel genetic material, which can come from adults or larvae. A positive result alone cannot prove that live baby mussels are present. Researchers combine DNA results with larval sampling and other observations.

12. One sample cannot tell the whole story. Scientists collect multiple samples to improve detection. A result with no detected DNA does not guarantee that the species is absent.

13. Try comparing samples from different places and depths. In this game, new water increases your survey coverage. Real eDNA does not form the perfect sampling spheres shown here.

14. Adult mussels can hitchhike on boats and equipment. Tiny larvae can travel in leftover water. Clean, drain, and dry gear before moving between lakes to help prevent spread.

15. Protecting a lake means protecting its other wildlife too. In this game, hydro blasts and scrapers kill fish. Chemical spray can harm fish, plants, and coral. Avoiding wildlife protects your score and the ecosystem.

16. Monitoring and prevention work together. Removing invasive mussels is only one part of protecting a lake. Collecting evidence and preventing invasive species from reaching other lakes are important too.
